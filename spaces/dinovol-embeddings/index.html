What these are, in plain words

An X-ray CT scan tells you how dense each tiny point of a scroll is. That is a lot of gray, and it is hard to ask a question of it like "where else does it look like this?". A model can turn the gray into something easier to compare.

The model

dinovol learned what scrolls look like

dinovol is a 3D model from the Vesuvius Challenge team, trained the DINO way: self-supervised, with no labels. Its main features come from learning which parts of a scan look alike, not from being told what papyrus is.

The output

Every small cube gets a list of numbers

For each 8 × 8 × 8 voxel cube (about 75 µm across) the model writes 480 numbers, and for each 4 × 4 × 4 cube it writes 64 more. Those lists are the embeddings, or features. Here they cover the whole scan.

Why it helps

Look-alike cubes get look-alike numbers

Each list has length one, so the dot product of two lists says how alike two cubes look: 1 is the same, 0 is unrelated. Click one point and you can find every place that looks like it, or train a small classifier from a few clicks.

In this slice the colors form bands that follow the rolled layers you can see in the CT. That is how the numbers vary here; it is not a check of the model.

Who it is for, and how to use it

  
Running dinovol over this scan took about 48 hours of GPU time. Now it is done once, and everyone can start from the result.

  

    
Concept search
Find everything like this
Pick a point, take its vector, and compute dot products over a region or the whole scan. High values mark cubes the model sees as alike.

    
Few-click segmentation
Train on frozen features
Label a handful of points as "this" and "not this", fit a logistic regression or a small MLP on their vectors, and apply it everywhere. No backbone to train.

    
Exploring
Cluster and map
Cluster a region, reduce it with PCA or UMAP, or compare scans of other scrolls you embed yourself with the same model and settings.

  

  
From nothing to your first numbers

  

    
Install three packages.
pip install "zarr>=3" fsspec aiohttp numpy

    
Open the store by URL. Nothing downloads until you slice an array, and then only the chunks you touch.
It worked when it prints (2448, 963, 963, 480) (4896, 1926, 1926, 64).

    
Turn stored numbers into vectors. Each vector is kept as int8 with one float16 scale: multiply them. Cubes outside the scroll are all zero.
v = g["tokens_unit"][i, j, k].astype("float32") * g["tokens_unit_scale"][i, j, k]
# i, j, k = z // 8, y // 8, x // 8 for tokens_unit; // 4 for dense and ink

    
Compare. A dot product of two vectors is their cosine similarity. The search box in Try it writes a complete example for the point you click.

How it was made

  
Every step ran from scripts on Hugging Face Jobs, with a record for each part. Nothing was edited by hand.

  

    
The organizers' model, pinned
Weights scrollprize/dinovol_8um_ps192_102126, teacher_step_640000.pt (sha256 2354c493…), run with villa's dinovol/dinovol_2/eval/embed_volume.py at commit 4ea26d9c on the dinovol-refactor branch.

    
One normalization for the whole scan
The input is the masked level-0 scan, 9.366 µm voxels. Its intensity statistics were measured once over 1,216 sampled chunks and used for every slab, so a vector means the same thing everywhere.

    
102 slabs on L4 GPUs
The scan was cut into slabs of 192 slices. Each ran on its own, 192-voxel tiles on a 96-voxel grid, each tile reading 32 voxels past the slab's edges. 22 Jobs on NVIDIA L4 GPUs took about 48 GPU hours. Tiles with no scroll in them were skipped.

    
One store, no rewriting
Each slab's chunk files were renamed into one scan-wide zarr (slab k is z chunk k), so the stored bytes are exactly what the model wrote, stored as int8 with a scale per vector.

    
Checked by URL, logged out
One 24 × 48 × 48 block from each of the 102 slabs was read back over HTTPS with no account: 5.6 million cells. No vector was off unit length by more than 1 percent, and the cells with features matched the cells with scan data exactly. This check could have failed; it shows the files are complete and line up with the scan. It says nothing about how good the features are.

  






  
Format

  
One zarr v2 group. Index i covers scan voxels [i × step, (i + 1) × step) on every axis, from voxel 0.

  

    
ArrayShape (z, y, x, numbers)One vector perStored as

    
      
tokens_unit2448 × 963 × 963 × 4808 × 8 × 8 voxelsint8, chunks 24 × 12 × 12 × 480, zstd

      
dense4896 × 1926 × 1926 × 644 × 4 × 4 voxelsint8, chunks 48 × 24 × 24 × 64, zstd

      
ink4896 × 1926 × 1926 × 324 × 4 × 4 voxelsint8, zstd

      
*_scalefloat16, one per vector: vector = int8 values × scale; after that each vector has length one

      
.zattrsThe model, its sha256, villa's commit, the tiling, the normalization statistics and the slabs present

      
index.jsonChunk file counts (156,480 per array) and the z range

    
  

  
Limits. These are raw model output, and nobody has checked them by eye. They are features, not a reading of any text, and the ink head's numbers are not an ink map. Each 192-slice slab was embedded on its own, so values can step slightly at multiples of z 192. Only PHerc0841 is here; for another scan, run villa's embed_volume.py with the same model.
