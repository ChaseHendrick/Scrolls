# Herculaneum book rolls and titles: research brief for the title hunt

Prepared for the title-hunt lane (branch `grok/laneJ-title`). Every factual statement carries a numbered citation to the source list at the end. Numbers marked "derived" are simple arithmetic on cited figures, not claims from the literature. Items marked UNCERTAIN or DISPUTED are flagged as such in the sources themselves or conflict between sources. Section 5 and `title-priors.json` are SEARCH PRIORS, not claims about what any unopened roll contains.

Scope note: several standard references requested for this brief (Cavallo 1983, Capasso 1991, Del Mastro 2014, Janko 2000, Obbink 1996) are print works not available in full online. Where their content could not be checked directly, this brief cites them only through what accessible reviews, catalogues or articles report about them, and says so.

## 1. Book rolls of the period (1st c. BC to AD 79)

### 1.1 Manufacture, kollemata, joins
- Papyrus stalks were cut into thin strips, laid in two layers with fibres perpendicular to each other, pressed so the plant's own starch bonded them, then smoothed; sheets were pasted end to end into a roll, which was rolled from right to left so it read from left to right [3].
- Herculaneum rolls were sheets pasted into lengths of roughly 8 to 16 m [1]; Mocella et al. note rolls could reach 15 m or more [3].
- The overlap where two sheets (kollemata) are glued is the kollesis. Janko advises measuring successive kolleseis and averaging, because kollema widths vary even within one roll [4]. He notes two clues to a kollesis: fibres are often disturbed where the glutinator pressed them down, and a vertical crack or break usually appears about 1 to 1.5 cm before the kollesis, which is often mistaken for the join itself [4].
- Many Herculaneum rolls were wound on themselves with no rod, so they have very small final circumferences; some had a central rod (omphalos, umbilicus) [4]. (Partial text of [4] only; treat details as indicative.)
- Successive circumferences of a rolled papyrus diminish fairly steadily towards the centre, a property Janko uses to reconstruct rolls [4].

UNCERTAIN: the PHerc. 330/332 figures describe a roll its authors call "fuori standard" (non-standard) and should not be used as typical [5]. Line spacing and typical column widths for standard rolls are in Cavallo 1983 and Capasso 1991 [11][12] but could not be verified online, so no numbers are given here.

### 1.3 Scripts and hands
- Cavallo's 1983 study of scribal hands allowed texts to be grouped and dated and showed that many separately numbered pieces belonged to the same roll [1][11].
- The On Vices books (PHerc. 222+223+1082+1675, 1008, 1424, 253) share one hand, and PHerc. Paris. 2 is by the same scribe, forming a fairly uniform edition [1].
- The final title of PHerc. 873 (On Conversation) is in the same hand as the front and back titles added to the uniform On Vices edition [1].
- Some copies of Philodemus' works were made after his death, and more papyri may date to the 1st c. AD than usually thought [1]. PHerc. 1667 is assessed as 2nd c. BC or possibly late 3rd c. BC, among the oldest in the collection [8].

### 1.4 Ink
- Herculaneum ink is traditionally treated as carbon based, which makes it hard to distinguish from carbonized papyrus in CT (context of [3][9]).
- Lead was detected in the writing of Herculaneum papyri and interpreted as intentionally added, probably as pigment or drier, rather than contamination [13][14]. UNCERTAIN: these studies do not show the composition is uniform across the collection.
- PHerc. 172's ink is unusually legible in CT; the Bodleian hypothesized a denser contaminant such as lead [15]. UNCERTAIN (hypothesis).
- The Scroll 1 (PHerc. Paris 4) expected title region has shown no detectable ink so far, "possibly a different ink" [7].

## 2. Titles

### 2.1 Positions and forms
- Ancient rolls normally had titles at both beginning and end [16]. Nearly all beginning-of-roll title pages from Herculaneum are lost; end titles are lost in many texts too [1].
- Del Mastro 2014 catalogues the titles of 118 Herculaneum papyri plus doubtful cases, treating initial, intermediate and final titles, the final protective sheet, graphic features, decorative and separation marks, and accessory data such as stichometry and number of kollemata [6][17]. (Content known via the catalogue record [6] and BMCR review [17]; review fetch intermittently fails.)
- In On Vices, beginning titles seem to have carried a fuller title and end titles a shorter one [1].
- Some end titles carry extra information: the number of columns or standard lines [1]; the term hypomnematikon appears in some end titles [1].

### 2.2 Layout of the end title
- Vesuvius Challenge, from opened rolls: the title often follows the end of the text in a subsequent, otherwise blank column; when the last text column is short it may sit below it. Typically the author's name is on its own line, then the title (possibly over several lines), then a book number line for multi-book works, sometimes followed by line or column counts; decorative strokes sometimes surround it [18].
- Typically author, title and number are each centred on new lines [2].
- Outside Herculaneum, end titles of prose authors give the author in the genitive followed by the title; titles are normally written larger and placed below the last line [19].
- Book ends in Roman rolls are typically marked with an end title plus a marginal coronis, often combined with a paragraphos [19]. Philodemus himself compares the end of his manic youth to "the coronis in a book" (AP 11.41.7) [1].

### 2.4 Frequency
- "Titles are common in Herculaneum scrolls" [2]; 118 titled papyri are catalogued by Del Mastro [6] out of a find of perhaps about 800 rolls [1]. No source located gives a percentage of rolls with surviving titles; the ratio 118/800 is derived and conflates rolls with inventory pieces, so do not treat it as a rate.
- A title can be missing physically: in PHerc. 1667 the title was not recovered and may have been on the lost upper portion [9]; in Scroll 1 the top rows of the expected title region are physically missing [7].

## 3. What rolls look like in CT

- Carbonized rolls are crushed and distorted; layer separation is hardest in the innermost windings, and letter sizes recovered by phase-contrast CT vary because of the folded geometry [3].
- The end title lies at the end of the text, i.e. the innermost part of the roll: PHerc. 172's title was found "in the very middle of the papyrus" [16][8] and in the innermost section [25]; PHerc. 139's title came from the final portion of the unwrapped surface [8].
- Ink first became visible in segmented 2023 scans as a "crackle" or cracked-mud texture [26][27].
- At 2.4 µm (ESRF BM18, phase retrieval) ink in PHerc. Paris 4 appears as bright, surface-associated deposits about 10 to 20 µm thick, directly segmentable in the volume [9]. 2.4 µm is described as the best resolution compatible with full-scroll imaging; 9.362 µm panels are "pixel-limited" [9].
- In flattened renders, ink sometimes appears directly, usually as bright areas, without a model [7].
- Available PHerc. 139 volumes: 9.362, 2.403, 2.399 and 1.129 µm [10]; PHerc. 172: 7.91 µm only, title recovered at 7.9 µm [15b].

## 4. Agent guidance for a title hunt

### 4.1 Expected letter sizes in voxels (derived)
Using the ~3 mm average body-text letter height [3] (titles are typically larger [19], factor not sourced):
| Voxel size | 3 mm letter (derived) | 10 to 20 µm ink layer (derived from [9]) |
|---|---|---|
| 1.129 µm | ~2660 vox | ~9 to 18 vox |
| 2.4 µm | ~1250 vox | ~4 to 8 vox |
| 7.91 µm | ~380 vox | ~1 to 3 vox |
| 9.362 µm | ~320 vox | ~1 to 2 vox |
The 2026 paper's 256-pixel window at 2.4 µm covers about 614 µm and was "smaller than all letters detected" [9], consistent with letters well above 0.6 mm.

### 4.2 Where to look
1. The innermost windings, after the last text column, in a column that may be otherwise blank, or below a short last column [18][16].
2. Expect centred lines: author (genitive), title, book number (possibly on the title line), maybe a count line [18][2][9].
3. Check both top and lower parts of the column; titles can be lost with damaged upper portions [9][7].
4. Look for decorative dashes or a coronis at the left of the last text column [2][19].

### 4.3 False positives
- Sheet switches: segmentation jumping between adjacent sheets [2].
- Fibre texture versus text: prize rules ask for rows parallel to horizontal fibres and warn that large model windows can hallucinate letterforms; validate on held-out regions [7].
- Kollesis cracks 1 to 1.5 cm before a join can mimic a vertical stroke or column edge [4].
- Phase-contrast fringes at interfaces (bright/dark halos) when propagation distance is long [9].
